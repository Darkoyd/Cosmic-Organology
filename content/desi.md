  - heading: What is DESI
    text: |
      The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument
      ([DESI](https://www.desi.lbl.gov/)) is an international scientific
      project exploring how the Universe has expanded over billions of years
      and trying to understand one of its greatest mysteries: dark energy.

      Located at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona, DESI uses 5,000
      robotic fibers to collect light from thousands of galaxies and quasars
      at the same time. By studying this light, scientists can determine how
      far away these objects are and map their positions across the Universe.

      The result is one of the largest and most detailed 3D maps of the
      Universe ever created, allowing us to explore the vast cosmic structures
      formed by galaxies and how they have evolved throughout cosmic history.

  - heading: "The Acoustic Fossil: Baryon Acoustic Oscillations"
    text: |
      In the early Universe, long before stars and galaxies existed,
      everything was extremely hot and dense. During this time, sound waves
      traveled through the young Universe, creating ripples in matter, much
      like waves spreading across the surface of a pond.

      As the Universe expanded and cooled, these waves left behind a lasting
      imprint. Billions of years later, we can still detect their traces in
      the way galaxies are distributed across space. Scientists call these
      patterns **Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO)**. These old echoes act as
      a cosmic ruler, helping astronomers measure how the Universe has
      expanded over time. We can no longer hear those primordial sounds, but
      their imprint is still written across the cosmos.

  - heading: Measuring the Expansion of the Universe
    text: |
      By studying these ancient patterns at different moments in the
      Universe's history, DESI helps scientists understand how the Universe
      has expanded over billions of years. One of the biggest mysteries in
      cosmology is dark energy, the unknown phenomenon believed to be
      responsible for the Universe's accelerating expansion.

      Recent DESI results, combined with other astronomical observations,
      [suggest that dark energy might be changing over time](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2025/03/19/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve/),
      rather than remaining constant as scientists have long assumed. These
      findings are not yet conclusive, but they open important questions
      about the history and possible future of the Universe.

  - heading: From Cosmological Measurements to Sensory Experience
    text: |
      **Cosmic Organology** takes the acoustic imprint measured by DESI as the
      scientific starting point for an artistic investigation into
      perception, instrumentation, and cosmic history.

      The project explores how cosmological quantities (including BAO
      distance measurements, expansion history and observational
      uncertainties) can inform sculptural structures that produce sound,
      vibration, and light.

      The installation seeks to translate the observational language of
      cosmology into an embodied experience. The aim is not to reconstruct
      the literal sound of the early Universe, but to explore how its
      measurable acoustic traces can become material for perception,
      interpretation, and artistic experimentation.
  - heading: "Open Data: The Universe for Everyone"
    text: |
      One of the remarkable things about DESI is that its scientific data are
      made publicly available, allowing researchers, students, artists, and
      curious minds around the world to explore the Universe.

      DESI's first major public data release
      ([DR1](https://data.desi.lbl.gov/doc/releases/dr1/)) includes
      **observations of more than 18 million astronomical objects**. These
      data contain information about the light coming from distant galaxies
      and quasars, helping us understand where they are, how far away they
      are, and how the Universe has evolved.

      Through **Cosmic Organology**, we explore how this openly shared
      scientific knowledge can move beyond research and become part of an
      artistic experience, connecting cosmic discoveries with sound,
      sculpture, and human perception. The data are freely accessible through
      the [DESI Public Data Portal](https://data.desi.lbl.gov/doc/).
